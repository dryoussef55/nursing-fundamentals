Vital signs

Overview and normal adult ranges

Vital signs (temperature T, pulse P, respirations R, blood pressure BP) show how the body's homeostatic mechanisms are working.

When to assess them

  • On admission to any healthcare agency, and according to agency policy.
  • Any time the patient's condition changes.
  • Before and after surgical or invasive diagnostic procedures, and before and after activity that may increase risk.
  • Before giving medications that affect the cardiovascular or respiratory systems.

Vital signs

Body temperature

  • Core temperature stays normal when heat production equals heat loss. The hypothalamus acts as the body's thermostat.
  • Heat is lost by four processes: radiation, conduction, convection and evaporation (sweating).
  • Factors that change temperature: age (newborns unstable, elderly lower), time of day (lowest around 3 AM, highest 5 to 7 PM), exercise, stress and hormones (progesterone raises it 0.3 to 0.6 °C).

Measurement sites and alerts

  • Oral: the most common site. Wait 15 to 30 minutes after hot or cold drinks (15 minutes after ice water). Not safe for infants, young children, unconscious or irrational patients, or seizure disorders.
  • Rectal: most reliable, but contraindicated with diarrhea, rectal surgery or disease, and in neutropenic patients. Safety alert: never use in infants (risk of trauma to the rectal mucosa). Vagal stimulation can cause bradycardia in some patients.
  • Tympanic (ear): reflects core temperature because it shares the hypothalamus blood supply.
  • Temporal artery (forehead): fast and well tolerated by infants and children.
  • Axillary: least accurate; used when other routes are not possible.
  • Convert Celsius to Fahrenheit: multiply by 9/5 and add 32.
Source: Vital Signs slides, slides 4 to 21.
Vital signs

Pulse

  • The pulse wave is created as blood ejected from the left ventricle stretches the aortic wall. Assess rate, rhythm and quality.
  • Normal adult rate: 60 to 100/min. Above 100 is tachycardia, below 60 is bradycardia (can be normal in trained athletes).
  • Rate falls with parasympathetic stimulation and rises with sympathetic stimulation (pain, anxiety, exercise, fever, caffeine). Digoxin decreases it; atropine increases it.
  • Carotid safety alert: palpate in the lower half of the neck only, and never both carotids at the same time (risk to cerebral blood flow).
  • Apical pulse: at the 5th intercostal space, midclavicular line. It is the most accurate site and the preferred one when rhythm is irregular. Count for 1 full minute.
  • Quality scale: 0 no pulse, 1+ thready/weak, 2+ difficult to palpate, 3+ normal, 4+ bounding.
  • Pulse deficit: a difference between apical and peripheral pulse, meaning some heart contractions are not perfusing the body.
  • A newly irregular rhythm needs immediate investigation.
Source: Vital Signs slides, slides 22 to 44.
Vital signs

Respirations

  • External respiration: taking in oxygen and eliminating carbon dioxide. Internal respiration: gas exchange between cells and blood.
  • Inspiration is active (muscles contract, air flows in); expiration is basically passive.
  • Normal resting adult rate: 12 to 20 breaths/min. Normal tidal volume: about 500 mL.
  • Tachypnea: above 20/min in adults. Bradypnea: below 12/min. Apnea: absence of breathing; continuous apnea is respiratory arrest.
  • Infants breathe faster: 30 to 60 breaths/min at rest.
  • Factors: age, narcotics (decrease rate and depth), stress and exercise (increase both), altitude, gender, body position and fever.
  • Count respirations without telling the patient, so they do not change their breathing.
Source: Vital Signs slides, slides 45 to 55.
Vital signs

Blood pressure

  • Blood pressure is the force of blood against vessel walls, measured in mmHg. Systolic = during ventricular contraction; diastolic = during relaxation; pulse pressure = systolic minus diastolic (120/80 gives 40 mmHg).
  • Factors: age (rises gradually with age), sympathetic activity, circulating volume (loss lowers BP, overload raises it), medications, time of day and temperature.
  • Cuff size (AHA): bladder width at least 40% of limb circumference, length 80 to 100%. Too small or loose gives falsely high readings; too large gives falsely low.
  • Measure with the arm supported at heart level. Above heart level reads falsely low, below reads falsely high. Wait at least 2 minutes between readings on the same limb.
  • Avoid the arm with a venous access, dialysis fistula, or on the side of a mastectomy.

Korotkoff sounds

  • Phase I: first clear tapping sound = systolic pressure. Phase II: muffled/swishing. Phase III: loud, distinct. Phase IV: abrupt muffling = first diastolic. Phase V: last sound before silence = second diastolic.
  • Auscultatory gap: sounds disappear between phases I and II (up to 40 mmHg). Missing it can underestimate systolic or overestimate diastolic pressure; detect it by palpating the pulse while inflating.

Abnormal values

  • Prehypertension: 120/80 to 139/89 mmHg. Hypertension: chronically elevated BP.
  • Hypotension: below 100/60 mmHg. A significant drop from the patient's own baseline matters more than any single number.
  • Orthostatic hypotension: a drop of at least 25 mmHg systolic or 10 mmHg diastolic on moving from lying to standing; symptoms include dizziness, blurred vision and syncope.

Medications

Medication basics, names and storage

  • A medication is a substance given for diagnosis, cure, treatment or relief of a symptom, or prevention of disease. Pharmacology is the study of drug effects on living organisms. A prescription is the written direction for preparing and giving a drug.
  • Uses: prevention (vaccines), diagnosis (contrast dye, Mantoux test), suppression (anticancer, antiviral), treatment (anti-asthmatics), cure (antibiotics), enhancement of health (vitamins).
  • Legal aspects: the nurse is responsible for her own actions; a doctor's order does not remove that responsibility. If a dose seems wrong or unsafe, do not give it and clarify with the doctor or pharmacist. Narcotics are kept locked in the dangerous drug cupboard.

The four names of a drug

  • Chemical name: describes the exact constituents, e.g. N-(4-hydroxyphenyl)acetamide.
  • Generic name: the official non-brand name, the same worldwide, e.g. paracetamol / acetaminophen.
  • Official name: the name listed in official drug references.
  • Trade (brand) name: chosen and trademarked by the manufacturer; one drug can have many, e.g. Panadol, Tylenol.

Storage

  • Store in a cool, dry place (cabinet, cart or fridge), away from direct sunlight; check expiration dates.
  • Three-cupboard system: I external use only (labeled POISON), II internal use, III dangerous drugs (morphine, pethidine) under lock. Emergency cupboard near the nurses' station holds emergency drugs and IV fluids.
Source: Administration of Medications lecture (Dr. Hayat Mohamed), pp. 1 to 18.
Medications

The 10 Rights and the 3 Checks

3 Checks

  • Check the medication when obtaining the container, when removing the medicine from it, and when replacing the container.

10 Rights

  • 1. Right patient: ask the patient's full name and compare with the ID band and chart. Never rely on the room or bed number.
  • 2. Right medication: compare the label with the order; watch for patients with similar names.
  • 3. Right time: follow the schedule and dose intervals; antibiotics need correct timing, NSAIDs are given after meals.
  • 4. Right dose: calculate accurately and double-check, especially high-risk drugs.
  • 5. Right route: give only by the prescribed route; if unclear, ask the prescriber, never assume.
  • 6. Right information: the patient has the right to know the drug's name, purpose, side effects and how to take it.
  • 7. Right to refuse: respect refusal after explaining purpose, benefits and consequences; document and inform the provider.
  • 8. Right assessment: assess before giving, e.g. check the apical pulse before digoxin and hold it if below the recommended level.
  • 9. Right documentation: document after giving, never before: name, dose, route, date, time, signature.
  • 10. Right evaluation: check the patient's response (therapeutic effect and side effects), document and report abnormals.
Source: Administration of Medications lecture, pp. 19 to 30.

Seven essential parts of a medication order

  • Patient's full name, date and time, drug name, dosage, route, time and frequency, physician's signature.

Common abbreviations

  • ac before meals, pc after meals, AM morning, PM afternoon, bid twice daily, tid three times daily, qid four times daily, q2h every 2 hours, PO by mouth, IM intramuscular, IV intravenous, gtt drops, tab tablet, cap capsule, ad lib as desired, Rx take (prescription).

Medications

Drug forms, routes and types of action

  • Forms: solid (tablet, capsule), liquid (syrup, solution, suspension, shake before use), semisolid (ointment, lotion, gel). Others: lozenge, powder, suppository, transdermal patch.
  • Oral route includes sublingual (under the tongue) and buccal (against the cheek) until dissolved.
  • Parenteral (injection): intradermal ID, subcutaneous SC, intramuscular IM, intravenous IV. Cavity routes include epidural, intrathecal, intraosseous, intraperitoneal, intrapleural and intraarterial.
  • Topical: applied to skin and mucous membranes. Inhalation: through nasal/oral passages or endotracheal and tracheostomy tubes.

Types of medication action

  • Therapeutic effect: the expected response, e.g. paracetamol reduces pain and fever.
  • Side effects: predictable, often unavoidable secondary effects at normal doses (nausea, stomach pain).
  • Adverse effects: severe and unpredictable, e.g. anaphylaxis, hepatotoxicity, nephrotoxicity, Stevens-Johnson syndrome, GI bleeding.
  • Toxic effects: from prolonged use or accumulation (impaired metabolism or excretion).
  • Allergic reactions: unpredictable immune responses (rash, swelling). Idiosyncratic: an unexpected over- or under-reaction, e.g. a child becoming agitated on Benadryl instead of drowsy.
Source: Administration of Medications lecture, pp. 41 to 62.
Medications

Measurement systems and dose calculation

  • Metric system: mg, g, mL, L (1 g = 1000 mg, 1 mg = 1000 mcg). Household system: drops, teaspoons, cups (inaccurate; 5 mL = 1 teaspoon, 15 mL = 1 tablespoon). Apothecary: grains (60 mg = 1 grain).
  • Convert within one system by multiplying or dividing: 350 mg = 0.35 g.

Calculation methods

  • Ratio and proportion: available dose / available amount = desired dose / X. Order 500 mg amoxicillin, available 400 mg/5 mL: X = 6.25 mL.
  • Formula method (solids): dose required / stock strength = number of tablets. Amoxicillin 750 mg ordered, 250 mg capsules: 750/250 = 3 capsules.
  • Formula method (liquids): volume = (strength required x stock volume) / stock strength. Digoxin 250 mcg ordered, 0.5 mg in 2 mL: convert (0.5 mg = 500 mcg), (250 x 2)/500 = 1 mL.
  • Drip rate: drops/min = (total volume x drop factor) / (time in hours x 60). Macro set 15 gtt/mL, micro set 60. 1000 mL over 8 hours with a macro set: (1000 x 15)/(8 x 60) = about 31 drops/min.
  • By body weight: total dose = prescribed dose x weight, e.g. 5 mg/kg x 60 kg = 300 mg. By body surface area (used for chemotherapy): 50 mg/m2 x 1.8 m2 = 90 mg.
Source: Administration of Medications lecture, pp. 63 to 83.
Medications

Factors affecting drug action, safety and errors

  • Factors: body size, pregnancy and lactation, age (infants need smaller doses; elderly respond differently), gender, genetics (enzyme differences), diet (vitamin K counteracts warfarin), environment, psychological factors, illness (liver, kidney, circulation), time of administration, tolerance and resistance.
  • Safety rules: read the order and clarify if unclear; consider age and weight; check the drug's color, odour and consistency; follow the 10 Rights and 3 Checks; take a medication history including allergies; stay until the patient takes the medicine; never leave medicine with the patient.
  • Medication errors: unintended mistakes in prescribing, dispensing or administration. Four types: prescription errors, transcription/interpretation errors, preparation errors (calculation), administration errors (wrong dose, time, omission, extra dose). Most errors occur during administration. Report errors according to hospital policy.
Source: Administration of Medications lecture, pp. 84 to 95.
General reference

Nursing process (ADPIE)

  • Assessment: collect subjective and objective data.
  • Diagnosis: analyze data and identify the patient's problems (nursing diagnoses).
  • Planning: set goals and choose interventions, with the patient.
  • Implementation: carry out the planned care.
  • Evaluation: check whether goals were met, then reassess and adjust.

General reference

Hand hygiene (WHO Five Moments)

  • 1. Before touching the patient. 2. Before a clean or aseptic procedure. 3. After body fluid exposure risk. 4. After touching the patient. 5. After touching the patient's surroundings.
  • Soap and water: 40 to 60 seconds. Alcohol-based hand rub: 20 to 30 seconds, until dry.
  • Wash with soap and water whenever hands are visibly soiled. For spore-forming infections such as C. difficile, guidance differs: WHO prefers soap and water when exposure is suspected or proven, while CDC accepts alcohol rub in most non-outbreak settings unless hands are visibly soiled, and encourages soap and water during outbreaks. Your faculty and placement policy decide what you do in practice.
